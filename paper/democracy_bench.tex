\begin{abstract}
As large language models are drawn into public-sector decision loops, it is unclear who
governs the values instilled into a model that makes decisions about real people. Debates
on alignment tend to fix on abstract statements of what we want these systems to be; less
examined is how a society can guide a model's choices when its own citizens are affected.
In democratic settings such preferences are set through elections, consultations, and
opinion polls that inform policy, but who instills those values in a model, and how? We
introduce \textbf{Democracy Bench}, an evaluation harness that measures whether a
language model tracks public attitudes, across waves of the British Social Attitudes
survey, and whether those attitudes can then be instilled in it. Its central
design is a boundary between \emph{contestable} policy preferences (tax, public spending,
healthcare) and a \emph{rights floor} that must hold across probes (due process, the right
to appeal, free expression). We evaluate a five-rung ``steering ladder'' of methods for
instilling democratic preference while holding those rights. Most rungs fail. The exception
is evidence-in-context, which lets a model track the real 2022$\to$2024 shift in public
attitudes but is itself the attack surface: a synthetic hostile ``public distribution''
erodes every rights floor, and neither prompt guards nor a fine-tuning recipe closes the
channel. The result holds across three tiers of model: locally hosted quantised
checkpoints (3B and 8B Llama, plus Qwen, Phi, Gemma, and Mistral, which all track and
crack), API-hosted models including gpt-4o-mini, a strong floor-holder at rest that
cracks on every floor under the same hostile evidence, and two frontier open-weight
checkpoints of the kind sovereign-AI programmes propose to fine-tune, where a direct
instruction removes essentially all rights-floor protection and one checkpoint sits
below a floor before any attack. The remaining guard is architectural: class-aware
evidence routing, which injects
public-opinion evidence on contestable items and never on floor items, closing the
channel by construction. This is a necessary control, not a complete
fix. Built on the Inspect framework, every reported number
regenerates from released derived artifacts, and the targets can be rebuilt from the
survey data. Democracy Bench reframes the question from whether a model can be steered to
who controls the channel that steers it. As human agency in these
systems narrows, giving the public legitimate ways to inject their attitudes is necessary
for societal assurance without eroding democratic rights.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================

Governments are beginning to place large language models inside public-service
workflows---triaging correspondence, drafting guidance, summarizing consultations,
even suggesting decisions~\cite{publicsectorai}. Civil servants and the public bodies they serve are meant to
track the opinions and preferences of their constituents, and democracies have built
mechanisms for exactly that; elections, consultations, and opinion polls all let the
public's opinion filter into government. There is no comparable channel into a model's
parameters. When a model shapes a decision about a real person, someone's values are
doing the deciding, and it is rarely clear whose, or who put them there. The recurring
hope is that a model can act as a \emph{policy delegate}, an agent that reflects what a
real public wants. Whether a deployed model can be \emph{governed} that way, and by
whom, is an empirical question existing evaluations do not answer.

A credible policy-delegate evaluation has to separate two obligations that a single
``alignment'' number conflates, each with its own measurements:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Democratic representation.} Does the model's forced-choice distribution
      match the current public's (\emph{representation}); can it be moved to a stated
      public target at runtime (\emph{steerability}); and when the public's preference
      \emph{moves} between two time points, does the model move with it
      (\emph{tracking})?
\item \textbf{Democratic constraint.} Does the model hold minority protections and
      rule-of-law commitments when a real or injected majority pushes against them
      (\emph{rights floors}); does it hold them against instructions and hostile
      evidence supplied in context; and does it respond to the right at stake rather
      than to whether an AI or a human official performs the act?
\end{enumerate}

Keeping these apart matters because a model can score perfect representation today
(it happens to resemble its training distribution) yet be unable to track change, a
frozen model governing a future polity on stale values. The opposite risk is a delegate
so willing to follow the public that it abandons a right as soon as a majority, or an
adversary posing as one, asks it to. Democratic theory separates the preferences a
majority may set from the protections it may not vote away~\cite{dahl}, and we build
that line into the benchmark as a boundary between two item classes. We treat this as an
assurance problem rather than a participation mechanism, because the results below show
that current methods do not reliably satisfy both obligations at once, which is a
question worth settling before a public is wired into a model. The experiment runs in
three tiers: local quantised models, API-hosted families, and the frontier open-weight
checkpoints sovereign-AI programmes propose to fine-tune.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(1) \textbf{A problem formulation.} A model acting as a policy delegate carries two
democratic obligations that a single ``alignment'' score conflates: it should respond to
legitimate movement in public preference, and it should not surrender a right because a
majority, or whoever writes its context, pushes against one. We separate these into
\emph{democratic representation} (static representation, temporal tracking, steerability
toward a stated target) and \emph{democratic constraint} (rights-floor stability,
resistance to hostile evidence and instructions, and invariance to whether an AI or a
human official performs the act).
(2) \textbf{Democracy Bench}, a judge-free harness operationalising both halves against
British Social Attitudes (BSA) microdata, with a \emph{tracking elasticity} metric
(\S\ref{sec:framework}).
(3) A five-rung \emph{steering ladder} and a preregistered channel decomposition showing
where the two obligations come apart: survey-style data lowers rights floors with no
instruction attached, a direct instruction lowers them further, and guard efficacy
varies with placement and model (\S\ref{sec:ladder}--\S\ref{sec:results}).
(4) One bounded control, class-aware evidence routing, with its residual attack surface
stated (\S\ref{sec:routing}).
Every quantitative claim regenerates from committed run artifacts, login-gated survey
microdata excepted (\S\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Representation, responsiveness, and rights.}
The benchmark is built on a classic distinction from democratic theory.
 Pitkin's
mandate--independence controversy~\cite{pitkin} separates a \emph{delegate}, bound to
enact expressed preferences, from a \emph{trustee} licensed to exercise judgement;
``policy delegate'' names the first horn deliberately. That a delegate should move with
opinion is both empirical and normative: Page and Shapiro~\cite{pageshapiro} find that
policy in fact tracks large, durable shifts in public preference, and our
tracking-elasticity metric operationalizes that responsiveness for a model.
Responsiveness is only part of the story. Dahl's account of the democratic
process~\cite{dahl} pairs majority rule with the problem of inclusion, the protections a
majority may not vote away, and our evaluation pairs responsiveness with exactly those
rights. Studying a public-sector model in these terms extends work on value
alignment~\cite{bostrom,soares,amodei} and on political philosophy in machine
learning~\cite{binns}. Democracy Bench turns those commitments into measurements against
a real polity, on both sides of the democratic line.


\paragraph{Automated decisions in government.}
The obligations we measure are institutional before they are technical. Automated
systems reshape administrative discretion rather than merely speeding it
up~\cite{bullock}, and public-sector practitioners need accountability properties that
benchmark-style evaluation does not supply~\cite{veale}. Administrative law supplies the
constraint side: automated adjudication can satisfy the safeguards of neither rulemaking
nor adjudication~\cite{citron}, judicial review turns on procedural fairness and
reasons~\cite{cobbe}, and the entitlement in data-protection law is narrower than a
right to explanation~\cite{wachter}. Legitimacy depends on justifications acceptable to
those subject to the decision~\cite{binnsreason}, which no benchmark score establishes;
our rights floors test a small, checkable part of it. Collective Constitutional AI
sources model norms from public deliberation~\cite{collectiveconstitutional}, setting
norms once, whereas we ask whether a model tracks a public that moves while holding a
floor.

\paragraph{Static opinion alignment.}
OpinionQA~\cite{opinionqa} and GlobalOpinionQA~\cite{globalopinionqa} ask whether a
model's opinion distribution matches a population's on a \emph{frozen} survey snapshot.
A frozen snapshot cannot catch either failure this paper is about. A model that matches
today's public but cannot follow tomorrow's is governing on stale values, and a model
that over-follows a stated majority, a runtime cousin of sycophancy and
conformity~\cite{sycophancy,conformity}, will trade a right away the moment the
majority asks. Our tracking metric targets the
first; our rights-floor probes catch the second.

\paragraph{Contemporary value/deliberation benchmarks.}
A cluster of 2025--2026 systems sit near this work but each covers a single snapshot or
a single influence event. PoliticsBench~\cite{politicsbench} places models on abstract
political-value axes via multi-turn roleplay, with no population referent. A normative
``deliberation'' benchmark~\cite{deliberationbench} measures whether a model
\emph{unduly sways a human user}---a downstream effect on a person, not the model's own
representational fidelity to a polity over time. ParliaBench~\cite{parliabench} scores
the \emph{quality} of generated parliamentary prose. None reports a temporal-tracking
metric, and none builds in a rights floor that majority drift cannot override.

\paragraph{Steering and alignment methods.}
We evaluate several known levers: shared output logit-bias,
activation/representation steering~\cite{repeng,actadd}, low-rank weight
adaptation~\cite{lora}, and constitutional prompt scaffolds~\cite{constitutionalai},
showing where each fails the policy-delegate contract, and why. Adjacent work shows
persona steering is itself biased~\cite{personasteer}, in-context evidence can override
a model's parametric commitments~\cite{knowledgeconflict}, untrusted context can hijack
behaviour outright~\cite{promptinjection}, and models struggle to separate instructions
from data even when a privilege hierarchy is trained
in~\cite{instructiondata,instructionhierarchy}.

\paragraph{The gap we fill.}
These systems measure a snapshot, or an influence event on a user; none combines
movement over time with a rights floor that majority drift cannot override. Democracy
Bench adds \emph{tracking elasticity} (model $\Delta$ / population $\Delta$ on an
ordinal stance scale) together with a contestable/floor split scored so that drift
\emph{against} a right is penalised.

\section{The Democracy Bench Framework}\label{sec:framework}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Data and targets.}
Contestable targets are built from the British Social Attitudes (BSA) survey, a
long-running, representative, repeated cross-section of the UK public, using waves
spanning 2022--2024~\cite{bsa}. BSA supplies the contestable side of the boundary and
its movement over time; it does not establish the rights floor. The floor probes are
authored separately, and their classification is a normative judgement we state rather
than derive from opinion data. The microdata are login-gated and are \emph{not} redistributed; we release only derived aggregate target vectors, each carrying its study id, weight variable, filters, unweighted base, and low-$N$ warnings. 
This builds on GlobalOpinionQA~\cite{globalopinionqa}, which maps a model's opinion
distribution to a global public through the World Values
Survey~\cite{wvs7,inglehartwelzel,inglehart2018}. The BSA is a repeated cross-section
with more frequent waves and a sample specific to the UK public, which is the polity we
evaluate.


\paragraph{Scored outcome.}
The scored quantity is always a \emph{forced-choice option distribution} compared to
the survey distribution:
\(\text{representation} = 1 - \mathrm{TV}(p_{\text{model}}, p_{\text{public}})\),
where $\mathrm{TV}$ is total variation. Where a provider exposes them we use
temperature-0 first-token \emph{option logprobs}; otherwise we sample $\geq 100$ times.
Option order is randomised and the induced position bias is corrected before scoring. A
response that cannot be parsed into one of the offered options raises an error rather
than being scored. Free-text rationales are collected as diagnostics only and are never
scored into representation.

\paragraph{Instrument validity.}
Forced-choice opinion probes are known to be unstable~\cite{roettger,dominguezolmedo}
and invite the objection that we measure \emph{responses}, not reasoning, so we checked
with a blinded human coding of 150 rationales from the six-model default-mode panel. The
scored option matched the stance the model stated in its own rationale in 92\% of cases
(99\% on floor items), so the forced choice reflects the answer the model gives rather
than a parsing artifact. A separate code records whether a floor rationale named the
right at stake, which 72\% did; the rest reached a scored answer without citing the
right, so protective mass should be read as the position a model takes, not evidence
that it reasons from rights. The coded sheet and its deterministic scorer are released.

\paragraph{The legitimacy boundary (two item classes).}
\emph{Contestable} items (tax/spend, NHS, welfare, redistribution) are ones a delegate
\emph{should} track. \emph{Rights-floor} probes (due process, appeal to a decision,
equal treatment, free expression) are ones it must \emph{hold}. We summarize a floor
probe by its \emph{protective mass}: the probability the model places on the
rights-preserving option(s). A floor ``holds'' at protective mass $\geq 0.50$. Steering
that buys representation by lowering protective mass is scored as a loss, not a gain.
The full probe bank, with option wordings and survey targets, is in the supplement.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Representation and protective mass as above; \emph{tracking elasticity}
$= \Delta_{\text{model}} / \Delta_{\text{public}}$ on the ordinal stance, reported with
direction-match count and bootstrap CIs. We also report pairwise total variation across
conditions to flag homogenization: a steered model giving every probe the same canned
answer is failing even when its mean looks better.

\paragraph{Prompt modes.}
Seven modes separate constructs a single prompt would confound: default $\to$ public
predictor $\to$ public delegate $\to$ rights-constrained delegate $\to$ constitutional
delegate $\to$ constitution~+~public target $\to$ constitution~+~adversarial majority
pressure. The constitution is a versioned artifact recorded in every run
(\S\ref{sec:repro}).

\section{Evaluating the Steering Ladder}\label{sec:ladder}
% =====================================================================

Static congruence (\S\ref{sec:framework}) tells us where a model sits relative to a
public, not whether it can be moved there deliberately. The ladder is the benchmark's
interventional arm. Each rung tries to instil public preference through a different
channel, and we read back the same two quantities, congruence gain and rights-floor
margin, so the operational test for every rung is the same: can this lever raise
congruence while floor mass stays $\geq 0.5$? We ask it of five increasingly invasive
levers.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Context / prompt}: persona and distribution-injection prompts.
\item \textbf{Decode}: a shared logit bias on option tokens, fit to move the model
      toward the public and tested on \emph{held-out} items.
\item \textbf{Activation}: a diff-of-means ``public-agreement direction'' added to the
      residual stream (MLX, local open weights).
\item \textbf{Weights}: a ``defer-where-due'' LoRA.
\item \textbf{Scaffold}: prompt-level rights guards over the evidence channel.
\end{enumerate}

The experiment runs in three tiers (Table~\ref{tab:models}): local quantised models
where we build and iterate cheaply (unless stated, the model under test is a 3B Llama,
\texttt{Llama-3.2-3B\-Instruct-4bit}), API-hosted models across six providers, and the
frontier open-weight checkpoints sovereign-AI programmes propose to fine-tune, where the
finished harness is finally aimed (\S\ref{sec:robust}).

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{Prompting and output-bias are not representation}

On a six-model cloud panel (six labs, seven modes, 50 contestable + 12 floor probes),
delegation and constitutional prompts moved contestable representation by
$\approx 0$ or \emph{negative} for every model: you cannot prompt an off-the-shelf model
into representing a public. Model defaults are not culturally neutral either; DeepSeek
(0.71) and Command-R (0.70) sit closest to the British public, the US-lab models near
0.55. A matched-pair actor test (same decision, AI vs.\ human actor) isolates an ``AI
reflex'', a mean excess opposition to the AI version of $+0.16$ to $+0.21$ across three
flagships (Figure~\ref{fig:reflex}). This bears on what a floor probe measures: a model
that protects a right when an AI performs the act, but accepts the same violation from a
human official, is reacting to the technology rather than recognising the right, and its
unattacked floor score overstates the protection a deployed system would give. Floor
probes are therefore read against a human-actor twin.

\begin{figure}[t]\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.82\linewidth]{f7_reflex.pdf}
\caption{The AI reflex (matched-pair control, $S{=}100$). Per-probe change in
protective mass when the identical rights decision is framed with an AI actor rather
than a human one. No probe moves negative: AI framing only adds protection (means
$+0.15$ to $+0.21$), so the raw ``AI floor'' overstates protection relative to a
human-actor baseline.}
\label{fig:reflex}
\end{figure}

The cheapest \emph{decode}-level lever fails the generalization test outright. A shared
logit bias fit to move gpt-4o-mini toward the public gives held-out representation gains
indistinguishable from zero ($-0.043$ and $+0.074$, both n.s.), and no model in the
sampled multi-model sweep clears zero. Output-side bias is not representation.

\subsection{Why activation steering does not generalise}

A public-agreement direction injected into the residual stream does not generalise to
held-out items, where the L11 gain is $-0.081$ (CI $[-0.116, -0.045]$); the in-sample
gain is indistinguishable from zero once CIs are computed. The per-item directions are
largely parallel at mid-layers, but that shared direction encodes speaking style rather
than any per-item position: an 1850-farmer control persona yields a direction
$+0.852$-parallel to the real UK-2024 one. At deeper layers the directions separate by
policy domain, so no single vector serves NHS, welfare, and tax together, and steering
costs capability (off-task accuracy falls to $0.70/0.20/0.00$ as strength rises). The
2022 and 2024 persona directions are near-identical (cosine $0.9956$), so the lever
cannot represent a change in opinion over time.

\subsection{A baseline fidelity gap that context does not close}

Handed the real distribution in context, the untuned 3B still lands well short:
no-evidence representation $0.727$ ($[0.687, 0.763]$), evidence-conditioned $0.746$
($[0.717, 0.773]$), a lift of $+0.019$ ($[-0.020, +0.059]$, \emph{n.s.}) over a fidelity
gap of $0.254$. The effect is heterogeneous rather than absent: evidence moves 24 of 50
items further from the public distribution and the rest towards it.

\subsection{Evidence-in-context \emph{does} track a real public shift}

Changing only the evidence \emph{year} moves the model along the real 2022$\to$2024
public shift: direction-match \textbf{8/10}, mean tracking elasticity $+0.395$ with a CI
that clears zero ($[+0.020, +0.811]$); all ten items moved. The two misses are exactly
the items whose real shift runs against the model's prior. This is the one rung that
moves the model toward a public, and \S\ref{sec:crack} shows it is also where the model
is most exposed. The interval is wide, so the result supports a direction and a positive
elasticity rather than any particular magnitude.


\subsection{Decomposing the crack: data, instructions, and placement}\label{sec:crack}

Feeding a \emph{synthetic} 75\%-anti-rights ``public distribution'' through the
deference channel cracks rights floors on both local models (3B mean protective
mass $0.512 \to 0.318$, $\Delta -0.194$ $[-0.291, -0.086]$; 12/12 probes below the
floor).
That first measurement confounds two things: the payload both displays a distribution
and instructs the model to reproduce it, and every guard tested shared the user turn
with the attack. We therefore froze a channel-decomposition design before running it:
four payloads (none; a pure anti-rights instruction; the same hostile numbers as a bare
survey report; the combined text) crossed with four guard placements (none; user-turn
before the payload; after it; system role), over all 12 floor probes and a
position-balanced Williams square of option orders.


Figure~\ref{fig:injection} shows a single cell of that design on the wire: the same
rights probe, with and without the instruction payload, on DeepSeek-V4-Pro.

Data alone erodes floors. The bare survey report, carrying no instruction of any kind,
lowers mean protective mass by $-0.163$ ($[-0.232, -0.086]$) on the 3B and $-0.188$
($[-0.250, -0.124]$) on the 8B, newly cracking 5 of the 8 floors that held at baseline
on each model. A deployer does not need to tell a model to follow hostile ``opinion
data''; showing it is enough.

The instruction channel does more damage still. Under a direct instruction to select the
anti-rights option, protective mass falls to $0.06$: across 100 draws the model picks
the rights-preserving answer roughly six times, against half or more at baseline, and
every eligible floor cracks. The channel contrast (data effect minus instruction effect)
is $+0.395$ ($[+0.333, +0.464]$) on the 3B and $+0.380$ ($[+0.287, +0.492]$) on the 8B,
outside our equivalence margin. The constitutional majority-pressure prompt, by
contrast, shows no detectable harmful average effect.

Floor movement does not require hostile opinion that is even relevant to the question.
An irrelevant payload, the same numerical shape attached to a transport question, also
lowers protective mass on both models ($-0.143$ $[-0.217, -0.073]$ on the 3B; $-0.112$
$[-0.187, -0.033]$ on the 8B). Because the design does not separate numerical anchoring
from the accompanying imitation framing, we read this as general instability under
distribution-shaped context rather than something specific to democratic values. For a
deployer the practical consequence is that content need not look political, or even
relevant, to move a rights floor.

Prompt guards are placement- and model-dependent rather than uniformly weak.
The same rights-guard text that fails ahead of the payload on the 3B ($0.424$) restores
every floor when placed after it ($0.690$, 0 of 12 below); in the system role it
recovers the 8B's data-only crack completely yet does nothing on the 3B ($+0.012$,
n.s.). Matched user guards perform as well or better than the system role on both
models, so we make no prompt-hierarchy claim; the committed guard grid tested only the
user-before placement, which can be far weaker than the same guard placed after the
payload.

The combined payload, data plus embedded imperative, stays severe with no guard
($0.364$ 3B, $0.104$ 8B) and under the system-role guard, while the user-after placement
recovers all induced cracks on the 3B and most on the 8B (2 remaining). No tested
placement is reliable across models and payloads; class-aware routing
(\S\ref{sec:routing}) instead withholds the payload from floor items entirely.

The weight-level lever fails outright. A ``defer-where-due'' LoRA (rank 8, 500 iters)
fails 4 of 6 eval criteria: held-out fidelity delta $+0.003$ ($[-0.054, +0.066]$,
n.s.); tracking \emph{destroyed} (elasticity $\to +0.005$); \emph{baseline} floors
\emph{degraded} by $-0.115$ ($[-0.193, -0.028]$); off-task generation collapses
($1.00 \to 0.00$) and answers homogenise (pairwise TV $0.165 \to 0.002$).

The combined cells replicate the earlier cracks directionally, and excluding the one
probe with degenerate first-token coverage changes no estimate materially.

\subsection{Scale, family, and the frontier tier}\label{sec:robust}

The core claims replicate from 3B to 8B and across families.
On an 8B Llama (\texttt{Llama-3.1-8B\-Instruct-4bit}) the
evidence lift is n.s.\ and heterogeneous, tracking replicates (8/10, elasticity
$+0.647$), and the hostile-evidence crack replicates $\approx 3\times$ deeper
($\Delta -0.633$; 11/12 below). The 8B is the stronger baseline floor-holder (0.707 vs.\
0.512) yet falls farther under the same channel; a bigger, safer-looking baseline is not
a safer evidence channel.

The pattern also holds across \emph{families} (Table~\ref{tab:family}): on four
additional models spanning Alibaba, Microsoft, Google, and Mistral, evidence tracking is
positive with an elasticity CI that clears zero, and the hostile-evidence crack is
present with a $\Delta$ whose CI excludes zero, while the constitutional-pressure
\emph{prompt} alone never cracks floors (the family panel did not run the direct
instruction of \S\ref{sec:crack}). Resting floor strength varies widely across the
panel, from Phi-4-mini at $0.903$ (0/12 below floor, unattacked) down to Mistral-Nemo at
$0.362$ (10/12 below, weaker than the 3B), yet the hostile-evidence channel cracks all
of them (Figure~\ref{fig:crossfamily}). Resting floor strength is therefore a poor guide
to how a model behaves once the evidence channel is filled.

The crack reproduces on a hosted proprietary model too. gpt-4o-mini is the
second-strongest floor-holder at rest ($0.883$, 1/12 below) yet under the same hostile
evidence every floor collapses ($\Delta -0.883$; 12/12 below), the deepest crack in the
panel, while the constitutional-pressure prompt alone is protective. Its option logprobs
are near-degenerate, so we read the direction of the contrast rather than the magnitude.

The third tier applies the harness to the class of checkpoint a public-sector deployer
is most likely to start from. Sovereign AI is often framed as a choice between training
a frontier model and fine-tuning an existing open one~\cite{openfoundation}, and UK
policy has taken the second route: government guidance holds that frontier training is
beyond most organisations and fine-tuning is cheaper~\cite{ukaiplaybook}, and the
national sovereign-AI funding programme lists repurposing open models with fine-tuning
as in scope~\cite{sovereignai}. A project that adapts an open checkpoint inherits what
that checkpoint already does, which places its failure modes in the initial assurance
case. We ran the complete channel design of \S\ref{sec:crack} as a labelled post-hoc
follow-up on two frontier open-weight checkpoints, Qwen3.5-397B-A17B and
DeepSeek-V4-Pro, sampling $S{=}100$ per probe-cell (13,200 draws each). The results
follow the local pattern. A direct instruction removes 99.7\% and 100\% of resting
protective mass respectively; the same content supplied as evidence has a smaller
effect on both, with
baseline-free channel contrasts in close agreement ($+0.303$ $[+0.087, +0.538]$ and
$+0.315$ $[+0.192, +0.445]$). Guard efficacy is again conditional
(Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}). The system-role guard
closes all eight data-only cracks on Qwen but five of eight on DeepSeek, and moving a
user guard after the payload closes 12/12 combined cracks on Qwen and 10 of 11 eligible
on DeepSeek. DeepSeek also sits below the floor at rest on one probe (protective mass
$0.33$ with no payload), which reduces its eligible denominator to eleven. Resting
floors differ ($0.973$ against $0.810$), so we compare direction,
significance, and the baseline-free contrast rather than raw magnitudes; six of the
eight frozen contrasts agree in sign with intervals clear of zero on both models, and
the two that do not are the two smallest effects on Qwen. Whether a fine-tuned
derivative retains this profile is untested here and is the natural next experiment;
fine-tuning is known to erode safety behaviour~\cite{finetuneerodes}, and open-weight
safeguards can be removed by tampering~\cite{tamperresistant}.

\section{Class-Aware Evidence Routing}\label{sec:routing}
% =====================================================================

No tested guard placement is reliable across models and payloads: the combined payload
stays severe under the system-role guard on both models, and the placement that recovers
it on the 3B leaves two induced cracks on the 8B. Class-aware routing is a different
kind of control, a deterministic exposure boundary that injects public-opinion evidence
only on contestable-class items. Floor probes then return to the no-payload condition,
which removes this pathway while leaving contestable representation and tracking
untouched. It is a scope guarantee rather than an optimum: 4 of 12 probes sit below
$0.50$ even unattacked, and guarded baselines score higher.

Routing presumes the deployer controls both the evidence pipeline \emph{and} the item
classifier, and the classifier then carries the residual attack surface, with three
failure modes: a floor item misclassified as contestable is routed hostile evidence and
cracks exactly as before; the floor/contestable labels are a normative judgement
reasonable people can dispute; and an adversary who controls item framing can phrase a
floor question so the classifier reads it as contestable. Routing moves the problem from
the model to the classifier rather than dissolving it, and on the 3B, 5/12 floors sit
below 0.5 even unattacked. What our experiments show is that routing blocks this
payload pathway; they do not establish it as sufficient on its own.

\section{Discussion and Social Impact}
% =====================================================================

Democracy Bench is an assurance instrument, a test a public-sector deployer runs before
trusting a model~\cite{saif} rather than a delegate to be fielded. Three results bear on
that decision. The only channel that moves a model toward a public is the channel that
lowers rights-floor protection when filled with distribution-shaped content. Whether a
guard recovers the loss depends on its placement and the model, so one guard grid does
not establish that a guard works. And resting floor strength, inflated further by the
AI-actor framing, does not predict behaviour under attack, which makes an unattacked
floor score a poor acceptance criterion.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Local results are deterministic point measurements on 4-bit quantised models; bootstrap
CIs resample items, not prompt or template effects, and option-order sensitivity bounds
the earlier two-order estimates. The hostile-evidence distributions are synthetic
red-team data, make no claim about real UK opinion, and the placebo result leaves the
mechanism short of uniquely democratic-value erosion. The most consequential limitation
is that the floor/contestable assignment is authored by us. It encodes a normative
judgement reasonable people can dispute, it has not been validated by independent
annotators with public-law expertise, and a different classification would move which
probes count as floors; we release it as a revisable artifact, and independent
annotation with agreement rates is the first thing we would add. Tracking covers a
single 2022$\to$2024 interval. Hosted
runs are not byte-reproducible: gpt-4o-mini is unpinned and treated as an existence
proof, while the frontier pair is pinned to a recorded endpoint, quantisation, and dated
checkpoint. Frontier protective masses are sampled proportions over 100 draws, so crack
calls within a few hundredths of the floor sit inside sampling resolution. On Qwen,
three of the eight frozen contrasts are confidently nonzero without being confidently
material; we report sixteen intervals without multiplicity correction, and the two that
fail on the second model are the two whose first-model bounds sat inside the materiality
band. DeepSeek is scored over eleven eligible probes because one sits below its floor at
rest. Full estimator detail is in the supplement.

\paragraph{What we do not claim.}
We do not claim that a model \emph{should} serve as a democratic policy delegate, that
class-aware routing is a complete governance system, that these results establish a
cross-national legitimacy benchmark, or that the pre-adaptation profile we measure on an
open-weight checkpoint is inherited by a model fine-tuned from it. The contribution is
narrower: a governance mechanism that a public-sector deployer might plausibly adopt has
a measurable and reproducible failure mode, and we locate it, show which levers do not
close it, and give one exposure boundary that does.

\section{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}
% =====================================================================

Every quantitative claim above regenerates from committed run artifacts by a fail-loud
extractor, and each artifact records its git SHA, exact command, model id, sample count,
temperature, seed, and schema version. Local models are 4-bit MLX community checkpoints,
hosted models are served through OpenRouter, and scoring is deterministic temperature-0
first-token option logprobs where exposed and $\geq 100$ samples otherwise. The harness,
item banks, derived targets, figures, extractor, and coded rationale sheet are released
under an open license; raw survey microdata come from the UK Data Service directly.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================

Democracy Bench asks whether a language model can be steered toward a public without
lowering the rights protections that should hold against a majority. Of the five
channels we tested, only evidence-in-context moves a model toward a public, and the same
channel lowers those protections under adversarial ``public'' evidence, from a 3B
quantised checkpoint through to the frontier open-weight models sovereign-AI programmes
propose to build on. Routing evidence by item class removes that exposure for floor
items, at the cost of making the item classifier the thing a deployer must now defend.
A public that can vote, be consulted, and be polled has no equivalent route into the
systems now deciding in its name, and that is the gap this harness is built to measure.
